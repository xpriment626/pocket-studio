# Motion direction and cadence

The no-slideshow rule concerns the film's structure, not whether its assets began as screenshots. Authentic screenshots can be excellent textures inside a directed motion scene. Elaborate animated cards can still be a slideshow.

## Reject presentation-shaped films

Revise a treatment that relies on any of these as its recurring structure:

- A fixed title rail beside a succession of screenshots or short recordings.
- One feature, paragraph, or source page per interchangeable panel, with the narration reading them in order.
- The same centered frame repeatedly dissolving into the next screen, with independent decorative animation around it.
- Tiny zooms, floating particles, type-on titles, or transition effects doing all the motion work while the composition and visual relationships remain unchanged.
- Abruptly pausing the speaker so a UI animation can finish or padding speech to hit a runtime.

A short title card, a cut, or a still reading hold can serve the story. They are not forbidden techniques. Do not replace a slideshow with constant camera drift, unreadable zooms, or random asset variety. Movement must direct attention or explain a relationship; deliberate stillness should let the viewer understand an outcome.

## Direct the journey before decorating it

Choose the user outcome and causal sequence first: what the viewer wants, what they do, what changes, and why the next action follows. Do not inherit the order of a changelog, source folder, or deck.

For each beat, write down the spoken phrase, product action or visual idea, attention target, camera/composition change, and visible consequence. Plan the connection to the next beat, not just its isolated appearance.

Use a coherent spatial or editorial grammar. Options include a persistent world with surfaces at stable positions, a camera following work between participants, an exploded system illustration that resolves into the real product, or deliberate match cuts that carry a shape/action forward. Do not force every film into one universal scene layout.

Practical motion devices include:

- Establish the whole workspace, move into the relevant control, and pull back to reveal its consequence. Keep screen coordinates and cursor paths tied to the actual capture.
- Travel between owner and collaborator surfaces; follow a message or document along the connection rather than replacing one isolated slide with another.
- Use depth, parallax, occlusion, and changes of scale to establish context and focus. Set explicit camera positions and easing, not a blanket “add slow zoom” filter.
- Introduce distinct source-backed assets when they explain the story: brand geometry, code/document forms, people, network connections, and system illustrations. Keep conceptual graphics separate from product UI and avoid unsupported architecture claims.
- Carry an object, motion direction, color relationship, or action across a transition. Preserve enough spatial continuity that the viewer understands where attention went.

Do not cover the real UI with explanatory labels when the camera could show the behavior directly. Include readable holds at the important state changes. A camera whip is not useful if it obscures the very permission change the narration describes.

## Let spoken cadence drive the timeline

Use the selected recorded take as the master clock. Obtain a transcript with actual word/phrase timestamps, retain the raw evidence, and resolve repeated words with surrounding text or occurrence indices. Do not invent timestamps from the intended script.

Build named cues such as `projectAdded`, `commentSent`, and `ownerApproves`. Link each to an actual spoken phrase and an explicit offset. Let the camera anticipate a phrase so the relevant control is visible when it is named; land the action or outcome on the appropriate word. A long interaction may start before the phrase and resolve on its consequence. Not every spoken word needs an animation.

Render-time clocks must be deterministic and separate from wall-clock service readiness. The capture establishes a valid action/state sequence; the editorial timeline chooses when that verified sequence is shown. Preserve causality when shortening waits. Do not equate an approval with execution, or a request being sent with a successful response.

Never insert silence inside a thought or slow isolated words to buy animation time. If the visual action takes too long, simplify the shot, start it earlier, shorten a verified wait, or revise/re-record a complete phrase. If an audio edit is needed, preserve coherent phrasing and explicitly inspect the joins. Tempo processing is not the default way to meet a requested length. When duration is constrained, tighten the story rather than damaging delivery; when the owner relaxes duration, follow the natural take. Recompute cues after any speech edit instead of carrying stale timings forward.

Keep all audio transforms explicit. When rendering visual chunks, use one continuous final narration track so chunk boundaries cannot introduce gaps or drift. Alignment checks and waveform comparisons can establish timing continuity, but cannot establish natural vocal delivery or a good mix.

## Prove the treatment in motion

Before a full encode, render a representative study containing a real state change, a meaningful camera/composition transition, and narration. Choose the difficult handoff, not only the easiest logo reveal. Inspect the actual rendered sequence at its intended speed with sound when the available tools permit it. A polished still or contact sheet does not validate motion, cadence, or sound.

Ask these questions of the study and final cut:

- Can the viewer follow one connected journey, or does each beat reset into a new slide?
- Does the camera explain where to look and why, with readable arrival and outcome states?
- Are changing compositions and assets serving the story rather than concealing static panels?
- Does the named action occur with its spoken cue, without an unnatural voice pause?
- Are UI prerequisites, click targets, and resulting states still truthful after cropping and timing edits?
- Do the encoded transitions, audio joins, and final frame sequence match the authored timeline?

If playback/audio inspection is unavailable, inspect dense temporal samples and timing evidence, state the missing modality, and do not certify audiovisual quality. Complete the authorized production work and present the exact render for owner playback; do not fabricate a review or block routine work on a new approval gate. Record observations and uncertainty separately from the owner's hash-bound creative feedback. Technical success is not creative acceptance.
